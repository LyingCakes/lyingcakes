## Stupid Questions Exist

Everybody knows everybody says there are no stupid questions but there are stupid questions and we know it. The problem is labeling them as a "stupid question" oversimplifies what is and is not being actually said. When people say, "this may be a stupid question but," 99% of the time in my experience the person asking is trying to expanding their understanding by trying to connect the new topic to a more familiar subject or piece of knowledge. The other 1% are truly stupid questions that are not worth asking. So, for this 99% the person asking must break the habit of labeling their questions that may be more tangential as pointless when any question that helps expand the breadth of the topic is worth asking. Any question that allows not only the person, but the recipient to expand their knowledge is a, smart question.

## ERROR 404 Something went wrong

My biggest advocacy for people learning new subjects is to be able to communicate when there is something wrong or something they do not understand. We have all been in a position where we feel completely lost as to what has just been said, and feel isolated in our limited understanding for us to throw our hands in the air and say "I don't get it, nothing makes sense". Something makes sense and being able to walk back your steps to your last point of understanding helps pinpoint the area of confusion. SMART questions must follow this idea.

<img width="800px" class="rounded float-start pe-4" src="../img/smart-questions/stack_overflow_good.png">

[StackOverflow Source](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/927358/how-do-i-undo-the-most-recent-local-commits-in-git)

One of the most highest rated Stack Overflow questions asked 17 years ago. The problem itself is minor and simple but the way the user went about asking is what is noteworthy. The user starts off by laying the context, the sitatution that led up to the point as well as what they have not done. This culminates into what is their actual question, backed by context, "How do I undo those commits from the local repository?", emphasis on the local. This question is clean and simple, while also maintaining its specificity. Without the emphasized local, it is unclear on which side of the equation are they trying to undo.

On the other hand, there are questions that provide exceptionally poor context.

<img width="800px" class="rounded float-start pe-4" src="../img/smart-questions/stack_overflow_bad.png">

Now, I am not an expert in IP configs. At all. But the situation the user provided does not make it clear as to what the problem or source of confusion is. The user does not specify on what it is they are using ipconfig. Maybe it is their home desktop, maybe it is a raspberry pi test bench, we do not know. Then the user is trying to make assumptions on what they see and trying to use StackOverflow to verify if their assumptions are correct, as opposed to looking up documentation. The post as a whole does not convey a problem that needs solving, it instead conveys a curiousity the user does not feel researching the answer for.

## Conclusion.

RTFM in my opinion is a lazy way of avoiding answering a question, especially since it is a hostile response. Sometimes the questions we ask are common knowledge, making it feel like a stupid question. But we must be sure we convey our lapse in understanding with an intent to learn and grow. It is okay to feel stupid, but our stupidity needs to be SMART. Our questions need to be beneficial for both us and the person who responds, but in order to do so must be asked in a way that helps certify its importance to why it is being asked. Providing context and intent helps with the determining the importance of the question, and is what we can use to help separate the 99% from the 1%. Sometimes there will be questions not worth answering, but every question can be made to be worth asking.
